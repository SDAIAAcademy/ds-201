Welcome. Two calibration questions: who has reported a p-value at work, and who could say in one sentence what it means. Expect many hands for the first and few for the second; say that the gap is the reason for this course. The rhythm of every session: about 25 minutes of ideas, one checkpoint, then a 25-minute lab on a population whose truth we can look up.

Today has no intervals and no tests. It builds the one idea they all rest on: a statistic changes from sample to sample, and the size of that change can be measured. Sessions 1 and 2 use household consumption, sessions 3 and 4 use repair times, which are skewed.

Do not mix them up

Three distributions carry three different spreads

The population

80,000 households, from 30 to 1,536 litres a day. Standard deviation 206 litres. Skewed to the right.

One sample

50 households. Looks like a rough copy of the population. Its standard deviation is near 206 too.

The sampling distribution

Means of many samples. Standard deviation 29.6 litres. Bell-shaped. You never see it in real work.

What goes wrong. A report quotes the narrow third spread as if it described households, or the wide first one as if it described the estimate.

Draw the three on the whiteboard one under another on the same axis. The third exists only because we can resample the population here. Tomorrow's bootstrap is a way to get an imitation of the third from the second alone.

The tool

A seeded generator makes every simulation repeatable

gen = np.random.default_rng(201)

def sample_mean(x, n, gen):
    s = gen.choice(x, size=n, replace=False)
    return s.mean()

means = np.array([sample_mean(x, 50, gen)
                  for _ in range(1000)])
  • One generator, passed along: the 1,000 samples differ, and the whole run repeats exactly.
  • replace=False: no meter is read twice within a sample.
  • The capstone asks for a seeded run log. The habit starts here.

FN-103 introduced default_rng and seeds; this is a reminder, not a lesson. The failure to name: creating a new generator with the same seed inside the loop, which gives 1,000 identical samples and a spread of zero.

A name for the spread

The standard error is the standard deviation of a statistic

  • It is the spread of the sampling distribution: 29.6 litres for the mean of 50 households.
  • It says how far an estimate typically lands from the truth.
  • Every statistic has one: a mean, a median, a difference, a slope.

You met a cousin in FN-103

The Monte Carlo error of a simulated average was a spread divided by √N. The standard error of a sample mean is the same idea, with households in place of simulated months.

One slide of recap, not a re-teach: FN-103 Day 4 covered the square-root law and the central limit theorem for simulation runs. What is new here is that the "runs" are households drawn from a real population, and that in practice there is one sample only.

The most common mix-up

The standard deviation describes households; the standard error describes your estimate

Standard deviation: 200 litres

"Households differ a lot: a typical household is about 200 litres away from the sample mean of 490."

Does not shrink when you read more meters.

Standard error: 20 litres

"Our estimate of the average, 490 litres, is typically within about 20 litres of the true average."

Shrinks with √n.

What goes wrong. "490 ± 20" printed without a word on which of the two the 20 is. Always name it.

Both sentences come from the sample of 100 in lab 1.2, step 5. Ask which sentence answers "how much water must the network deliver to one street?" (the first) and which answers "is this year's average different from last year's?" (the second).

Where this leads

Estimate ± 2 standard errors is the first interval

  • In lab 1.1, 94% of sample means lay within 57 litres of the truth: about 2 standard errors.
  • Turn it around: the truth lies within 2 standard errors of most sample means.
  • For the sample of 100: 490 ± 2 × 20, from 450 to 530 litres. The truth, 470.4, is inside.

Not every statistic has a formula

The median's standard error at n = 100 is 25.5 litres by simulation (lab 1.2, stretch). No σ/√n gives it. Day 2 gets it from one sample by resampling.

Do not call it a confidence interval yet and do not define 95%: Day 2, session 2 does that and tests the claim. The point here is the direction of the argument, from "samples land near the truth" to "the truth is near my sample".

A different kind of data

Repair times are skewed: most are short, a few are very long

All 12,000 leak repairs of the year.
  • Median 6.5 hours, mean 9.9 hours.
  • 70% of repairs are shorter than the mean.
  • Durations, costs, waiting times and consumption in litres are usually skewed like this.

Population numbers from repairs.csv. Ask which of the two numbers a customer would call "typical". When seven repairs in ten are shorter than the "average", the average is a poor word for typical.

Two meanings of "the middle"

The mean is a balance point; the median is the middle ticket

Mean

Add all hours, divide by the count. Every hour of every ticket counts, so one long ticket moves it. Mean × count = total hours.

Median

Sort the tickets, take the middle one. Only the order counts, so a ticket of 50 hours and one of 2,000 hours pull equally.

Sample of 200 regular repairs (lab 1.3): mean 8.11 hours, median 6.40 hours, 63% of repairs shorter than the mean, longest 48.4 hours.

The line "mean times count equals total" matters later in the session: it is the reason the mean cannot simply be abandoned.

Skew makes the mean a harder target for inference too

  • The median has no σ/√n formula. Lab 2.1 gets its interval by resampling.

By district only, and not when the count is small

Statistics: a median of 8 tickets jumps with every new ticket.

The line between today and tomorrow

A description is about the sample; an inference is about the population

Description, today

"In these 300 repairs the median was 6.2 hours." True by arithmetic. No uncertainty needed.

Inference, from tomorrow

"The median of all 12,000 repairs is between two stated values." A claim that can be wrong, and whose error rate can be measured.

The true median is 6.5 hours. The sample said 6.2. Tomorrow's interval has to be wide enough to admit 6.5, and you will count how often it is.

Close the content of Day 1 here. The move from the left card to the right card is the whole subject of the next three days.

Day 1 in three lines

What to keep

A statistic varies

One sample is one draw of many. The sampling distribution shows all the draws you did not get.

The standard error measures it

The spread of a statistic, s / √n for a mean, from one sample. Not the standard deviation of households.

Describe before you infer

Median and quartiles for skewed data, the count, the exclusions, the population.

Ask one participant for the difference between a standard deviation and a standard error in one sentence each. If it does not come easily, start Day 2 with the checkpoint of session 2 again.
