Review of Day 2

Which interval would you report?

A survey phoned 50 households in each of 8 districts chosen at random. Bootstrapping the 400 rows gives a 95% interval of 6.5 to 6.8; bootstrapping the 8 districts gives 6.3 to 7.0. Which do you report, and why?

6.5 to 6.8, because it is more precise
6.5 to 6.8, because it uses 400 observations and not 8
6.3 to 7.0, because districts were the unit drawn at random
Something in between, to be safe
Why. Resample the unit that was sampled. In lab 2.3 the row bootstrap covered the truth in 72% of surveys, the district bootstrap in 94%.

Answer: C. The same rule governs today's shuffles: what you shuffle must be what could have been assigned differently.

The null hypothesis

If the contractor makes no difference, the labels are arbitrary

1 · Assumethe contractor has nothing to do with the score
→
2 · Shufflethe 800 labels: any 400 could have been "A"
→
3 · Recomputethe difference in means under the shuffled labels
→
4 · Repeat5,000 times and see where 0.295 falls
The null hypothesis is a rule for generating data. Here the rule is: shuffle the labels. No formula is needed to state it.

This is the central slide of the day. A null hypothesis is usually written as an equation between two population means, which hides what it does. As a shuffling rule it is a machine you can run. Every test in the course is this machine with a different rule.

Lab 3.1, steps 3 and 4

Under shuffled labels a gap of 0.295 is rare

5,000 shuffles of the contractor labels.
  • Centred on zero, spread 0.135.
  • The largest shuffled gap in 5,000: 0.525.
  • Gaps of 0.295 or more, in either direction: about 31 in every 1,000 shuffles.
  • That share is the p-value: 0.031.

Two-sided because a gap in B's favour would have interested Nizar as much. The p-value counts the observed arrangement as one of the shuffles: count plus one over 5,001. Either the contractors differ, or something that happens 3 times in 100 has happened.

Lab 3.1, step 5

The t-test is a formula for the histogram you just drew

by shufflingby formula (Welch)
spread of the difference under the null0.13460.1353
p-value0.03140.0295
  • t = difference / standard error = 0.295 / 0.1353 = 2.18.
  • The t distribution is the bell curve that the shuffles trace out.
  • With 400 per group the approximation is excellent.

The classical tests were invented when 5,000 shuffles were impossible, as approximations of exactly this count. They are still worth knowing because every library and every report uses them. Welch's version does not assume equal spread in the two groups and is the safe default.

Checkpoint

What does one shuffle assume?

A pilot gave 60 customers a new bill layout and 60 the old one, by lot. To test the difference in mean hold time, the analyst shuffles the 120 layout labels. What does each shuffle take for granted?

That the layout has no effect, so any 60 customers could carry the new label
That the new layout shortens hold time for every customer
That hold times follow a normal distribution
That the 120 customers are replaced by new ones each time
Why. If the label does not matter, every assignment of it is as likely as the real one.

Answer: A. Option D separates the bootstrap from the permutation test: the bootstrap resamples units with replacement to imitate new samples; the permutation test keeps the data and reassigns labels without replacement to imitate "no effect".

Steps 1 to 4 without an assistant. The loop of 5,000 shuffles takes a few seconds. Watch for a one-sided count where the lab asks for two-sided, and for shuffling the scores and the labels both, which is harmless and confusing. The lab loads the supplied study file and needs nothing from Day 2.

One sentence to keep

A p-value is the share of null worlds at least as extreme as yours

"If the two contractors were really the same, a difference at least as large as the one we observed would turn up in about 3 studies in 100."

It starts with "if"

The null is assumed, not assessed.

It is about data

How unusual this result is, not how likely a hypothesis is.

It knows nothing of size

Small gap, huge sample: tiny p-value.

This sentence is sentence 3 of the marked sheet, one of the three that are right. Have the room say it aloud with the "if". Every misreading on the next slide drops the "if" or changes what the 3% is a share of.

What reports get wrong

Five misreadings turn up again and again

The misreadingWhy it is wrong
"p = 0.03: a 3% chance of no effect"The 3% is computed assuming no effect
"p = 0.40: proves no effect"A small study gives a large p-value too
"p = 0.001: a large effect"A p-value mixes effect size with sample size
"The 95% interval for the mean holds 95% of the repairs"The interval is about the mean, not about single repairs
"30 tests, 2 significant: each has a 5% chance of being false"With 30 tests, one or two false alarms are expected

These are five of the eight sentences of lab 3.2, so do not project this slide while participants mark the sheet: show it before the lab, then hide it. The catalogue of such misreadings by Greenland and colleagues lists twenty-five; these five are the ones a utility report contains. Sources: Greenland and others (2016).

Lab 3.2, step 3

Near p = 0.03, the null was true in 13% of studies, not 3%

13%
of the 248 studies with p between 0.02 and 0.04 had two identical groups
  • The share depends on what the p-value cannot know: how many tested hypotheses are true to begin with (here half), and how well the study detects a real gap.
  • If only one hypothesis in ten were false, the share would be far higher.
  • A p-value alone can never be "the chance that the null is true".

This is the cleanest refutation of the most common misreading: count it. Ask the room to predict the number before revealing it. Change the mix to nine true nulls for each false one and most small p-values come from true nulls.

Lab 3.2, step 4 and stretch

When nothing is going on, every p-value is equally likely

  • The 2,000 p-values under a true null fall evenly between 0 and 1: about 200 in each tenth.
  • So 5% fall below 0.05, by construction. That is all "significant at 0.05" guarantees.
  • In batches of 30 such tests: 1.38 flagged on average; at least one flag in 80% of batches.

Sentence 7, counted

"30 districts tested, two significant, each with a 5% chance of being a false alarm." In batches where nothing differs, two or more flags appeared 41% of the time, and every one of them was false.

The counts in ten equal bins in the solution run were between 182 and 227. This slide is the bridge to session 4. The threshold 0.05 is a convention about how many false alarms to tolerate per test; it says nothing about a set of tests.

In a report

Write what the p-value says, then give the reader what it cannot

Before

"The difference between the contractors gave p = 0.03, so there is a 3% chance that the two contractors are really the same."

After

"Contractor A was rated 0.29 points higher (95% interval 0.03 to 0.55). If the contractors were the same, a gap this large would turn up in about 3 studies in 100."

Order of information: the estimate, its interval, then the test. The p-value is the last thing a manager needs, not the first.

Which reading of the result is sound?

A trial of a new valve schedule in one district compares 40 days with it and 40 days without. Mean daily bursts fell from 3.1 to 2.8; p = 0.38. Which sentence may go in the report?

The schedule has no effect on bursts
There is a 38% chance that the schedule does nothing
The schedule cut bursts by 10%

Why. A large p-value says the data are compatible with no effect. It does not say the effect is zero; the interval shows what remains possible.

After "is there a gap?"

The useful question is how big, and whether that matters

  • The test said the contractors differ. Nizar's contract says a gap of 0.5 points or more is material and triggers a review.
  • So the decision hangs on the size of the gap, with its uncertainty.
  • An effect size is the gap in units someone can judge.

Statistical significance

The gap is unlikely to be exactly zero.

Practical significance

The gap is large enough to act on. The threshold comes from the business, never from the data.

The threshold of 0.5 points is part of the case: a clause in Zulal's contracts. In the capstone every participant has to find or argue such a threshold for their own question; the conservation-message question gives one, the others leave it to argue.

Lab 3.3, step 1

Report the raw effect for the decision, the standardised one for comparison

Raw effect: 0.295 points

In the units of the scale. This is what the contract's 0.5 is written in.

Standardised effect: d = 0.154

The gap divided by the standard deviation of the scores, 1.91. "About a sixth of the spread of single households' scores."

  • d = 0.15 means the two score distributions overlap almost completely.
  • d helps compare across scales and studies. It does not say whether the gap matters.

The standardised effect is known as Cohen's d. Its conventional labels, small, medium and large, are a last resort for when no practical threshold exists. Here one exists, so the raw effect decides.

Lab 3.3, steps 2 and 3

Place the interval against zero and against the threshold

Bootstrap interval for the satisfaction gap, 400 responses per contractor.
  • Excludes 0: the gap is real.
  • Includes 0.5: material or not is not settled.
  • The estimate, 0.29, is below the threshold.

The honest answer to Nizar: "A is rated higher. Whether the gap reaches your threshold, this study cannot say; the best estimate is below it." The truth, 0.462, is inside the interval and below the threshold.

A reading rule

An interval can sit in four positions, and each has its own sentence

The intervalThe sentence
includes 0, lies below the thresholdNo gap shown; a material gap is ruled out
excludes 0, lies wholly below the thresholdA real gap, and not a material one
excludes 0, includes the thresholdA real gap; material or not is not settled
lies wholly above the thresholdA real and material gap

A fifth case, an interval that includes both 0 and the threshold, settles nothing: the study was too small for the question.

The highlighted row is today's case. A p-value distinguishes only "includes 0" from "excludes 0": two of these four positions get the same p-value verdict and opposite decisions.

What can go wrong

A test can err in two directions

The test says "gap"The test says "no gap shown"
In truth, no gapType I error: a false alarm. Rate set by the threshold, 5%Correct
In truth, a gapCorrect. Its rate is the powerType II error: a miss. Rate = 1 - power
  • The 5% is chosen. The power is not: it depends on the size of the true gap and on the sample size.
  • In lab 3.2, groups of 100 detected the true gap of 0.46 in 42% of studies: they missed it more often than not.

A study with low power is the origin of "p = 0.40 proves no effect": it would have said "no gap shown" most of the time whether or not there was one. Power is asked for before a study and estimated here by simulation, without a formula.

Lab 3.3, step 5

Three sentences: the estimate, the test, what is settled

"In a study of 400 responses per contractor, households rated contractor A 0.29 points higher than contractor B on the 10-point scale (95% interval 0.03 to 0.55). A gap of this size would be unusual if the contractors were the same, so we conclude that A is rated higher. The solution's wording. The last clause is a limits statement: the population the conclusion applies to. Tomorrow's brief adds a description and, where needed, a regression around this core.

The quarterly audit

Ilyas tests all 30 districts every quarter

  • An audit sample of 4,000 repairs. For each district: is its repair time different from the others?
  • Repair times are skewed, so the test compares log hours: the estimate is a ratio of typical repair times.
  • Districts flagged at p < 0.05 get an inspection visit.

The truth, on file

Five districts truly differ: three slower by about 20%, two faster by about 14%. Twenty-five do not. You open the list after testing, not before.

The planted districts are D05 (ratio 0.87), D16 (1.22), D19 (1.22), D21 (1.20) and D29 (0.85), of very different sizes. Do not show the list before step 2 of the lab.

The same problem without a list of tests

Every choice made after seeing the data is another test

  • Mean or median? All tickets or closed ones? This quarter or the last two? Districts or zones?
  • An analyst who tries a few and reports the one that "works" has run many tests and shown one.
  • Nobody need cheat: each choice seems reasonable in the light of the data.

The remedy: a plan before the data

Write down the question, the estimand, the unit, the test and the list of comparisons first. Report every comparison on the list, and mark anything added later as exploration.

Gelman and Loken call this the garden of forking paths. The capstone rubric asks for the count of comparisons run, including those not reported; lab 4.4 has participants write their estimand and unit before they open the capstone data. Sources: Gelman and Loken (2013).

Lab 3.4, step 3

Two corrections make two different promises

Holm: family-wise error

"The chance of even one false flag among the 30 stays below 5%."

Sort the p-values. Compare the smallest with 0.05/30, the next with 0.05/29, and so on. Stop at the first that fails.

Benjamini-Hochberg: false discovery rate

"Among the districts flagged, the expected share of false ones stays below 5%."

Compare the k-th smallest p-value with 0.05 × k/30. Flag everything up to the last one that passes.

from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests
reject = multipletests(p, alpha=0.05, method="holm")[0]      # or method="fdr_bh"

Holm by hand on this audit: 0.00000 against 0.00167, 0.00008 against 0.00172, 0.00088 against 0.00179 all pass; 0.00301 against 0.00185 fails, so Holm flags three districts. Holm is never worse than the simpler rule of dividing 0.05 by 30 for every test. Holm's promise holds whatever the relation between the tests. Benjamini-Hochberg's is proven for independent or positively related tests; "each district against the others" are related, and the count on the next slide is the check that it held here.

Lab 3.4, step 5

The smallest districts give the most extreme estimates by chance

One audit: estimated ratio of repair time, district against the others.
  • Districts with no true difference and under 60 audited repairs are off by 11% on average; those with 150 or more by 6%.
  • A league table sorted by estimate puts small districts at both ends, different ones each quarter.
  • Before ranking units of unequal size, plot the estimate against the size.

The funnel is the responsible-use point of the course: a "worst district" list built on raw estimates mostly names small districts, and in a small district a number is close to pointing at households. The smallest planted district sits among the noise and cannot be told apart at this sample size.
