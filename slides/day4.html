Review of Day 3

How many of these flags can you trust?

A monthly report tests 20 service counters against the average at the 0.05 level and flags one. No correction is applied. What is the fair reading of the single flag?

It may well be a false alarm: 20 tests of counters that do not differ give one flag on average
It has a 5% chance of being a false alarm
The counter certainly differs from the others
It must be ignored, since uncorrected tests mean nothing
Why. 20 × 0.05 = 1 false flag expected when nothing differs. One flag in twenty is what chance alone delivers.

Answer: A. Two minutes. The follow-up question: what would you do next? Apply Holm, or give the flagged counter a second, independent look.

Lab 4.1, steps 1 and 2

A fitted line is a summary of the group averages

2,000 households: mean consumption at each household size, and the least-squares line.
  • From one size to the next, the mean rises by 88 to 107 litres.
  • One line replaces twelve means with two numbers: where it starts and how steeply it climbs.
  • consumption = 63.7 + 98.48 × people.

Solution run of lab 4.1. The dots for 10, 11 and 12 people rest on 9, 6 and 2 households: they wander, and the line rightly gives them little weight. Ask why a line and not the twelve means: it is simpler to say, steadier where data are thin, and it can be given an interval.

How the line is chosen

Least squares picks the line with the smallest squared misses

def fit_line(x, y):
    dx, dy = x - x.mean(), y - y.mean()
    slope = (dx * dy).mean() / (dx ** 2).mean()
    intercept = y.mean() - slope * x.mean()
    return intercept, slope
  • slope = covariance(x, y) / variance(x).
  • The line always passes through the point of the two means.
  • The same line as np.polyfit(x, y, 1), and as the projection of FN-103.

One slide of recap: FN-103 Day 2 derived least squares as a projection and solved the normal equations. Nothing of that is repeated. What is new from here on is inference about the slope.

What the line does not say

"Goes with" is not "causes"

What was compared

Different households of different sizes, at one moment.

What was not observed

One household before and after a person moved in.

  • Households of different sizes may differ in other ways too: where they live, the kind of home, a garden.
  • Whatever travels with household size is folded into the 98 litres. Session 3 finds one such thing.

Keep the wording discipline from now on: "one more person goes with", "households with one more person use on average". The planted confounder is temperature; do not name it yet.

What does this slope tell the planner?

Slopes with intervals

02
Day 1, for a slope

A slope has a sampling distribution and a standard error

  • Your sample says 98.48 litres per person. The population says 96.21.
  • Everything from Days 1 and 2 applies: the slope's spread over samples is its standard error, and an interval is built from it.
  • Two routes again: a formula in the library, and a bootstrap.
             coef  std err      t  [0.025  0.975]
Intercept  63.739    5.160  12.35  53.620  73.858

The table is the statsmodels summary from the stretch of lab 4.1, with the p-value column (0.000 in both rows) left out to fit and household_size shortened to size. Walk the columns: coefficient, standard error, t as their ratio, the 95% interval. The reported interval, 96.27 to 100.69, does not contain the population slope 96.21. One miss proves nothing; this session counts.

Lab 4.2, step 2

Bootstrap the households: each one keeps its pair of values

idx = gen.integers(0, n, size=(B, n))
xs, ys = x[idx], y[idx]   # pairs stay together
xc = xs - xs.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
yc = ys - ys.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
num = (xc * yc).mean(axis=1)
slopes = num / (xc ** 2).mean(axis=1)
  • The unit drawn at random is the household, so the household is resampled: its size and its consumption together.
  • Resample x and y separately and the relation is destroyed: every slope comes out near zero.
  • 2,000 slopes: standard deviation 1.409.

The same index array picks both columns, which is the whole point. This "pairs bootstrap" makes no assumption about how the points scatter around the line.

Lab 4.2, step 4

The formula assumes equal spread around the line; the data do not have it

Standard deviation of the residuals at each household size.
  • Households of 8 scatter twice as widely around the line as households of 1: 133 against 67 litres.
  • Large households sit at the end of the line, where a point has most pull on the slope, and they are the noisiest.
  • The formula averages the noise over everyone and understates the swing.

The textbook word is heteroscedasticity; "unequal spread" does the job. It is the rule for quantities that grow: bigger households, bigger customers, bigger bills all vary more.

Lab 4.2, step 5

Counted over 300 samples, the bootstrap interval keeps the promise

reported interval
92.0%

95.0%

95%
  • Libraries can drop the assumption too: the robust standard error (HC3) is 1.402, the bootstrap's 1.409.
  • When the reported and the robust standard errors differ, report the robust one and say why.

300 samples of 2,000 drawn from the population, 500 resamples each. With 300 samples the counting error is about 2.5 points, so 92% is a shortfall at the edge of what the count can show; the direction agrees with the standard errors. The robust standard error is one argument to the library and costs nothing.

In practice

Read a regression table in this order

  1. The coefficient, with units: 98 litres a day per person.
  2. Its interval: 95.7 to 101.2 by bootstrap. Is the whole interval large enough, or small enough, to matter?
  3. Where the standard error comes from: the default formula, a robust one, or a bootstrap.
  4. The p-value last: it tests "slope is exactly zero", which is rarely the question.

The same order as yesterday's report: estimate, interval, then test. Nobody doubted that bigger households use more water; the t of 87 answers a question nobody asked.

Checkpoint

Which standard error goes in the brief?

A regression of monthly bill on meter size gives a slope of 41 riyals per size step. The library reports a standard error of 2.0; a bootstrap of customers gives 3.1. A plot shows bills scattering far more widely for large meters. What should the analyst report?

2.0, because the library's formula is the standard method
3.1, because the spread is unequal and the bootstrap does not assume otherwise
About 2.5, the middle of the two
Neither: the slope cannot be trusted when spreads differ
Why. Unequal spread does not bias the slope; it makes the default standard error too small. The bootstrap, or a robust standard error, is the honest one.

Answer: B. Option D confuses two things worth separating: the slope itself is still a fair estimate; only its stated uncertainty was wrong.

Session 3

Residuals and confounding

  • Read a residual plot for curvature and unequal spread
  • Add a second variable and explain why the first slope moved
  • State what a regression on observed data cannot settle
03
What the line left behind

A residual is what the model missed, and patterns in residuals are evidence

No pattern

A level band around zero: the model has captured what is systematic.

A trend or a curve

Against some variable the residuals rise, fall or bend: something systematic is missing.

A funnel

The band widens: unequal spread. The slopes stand, the default standard errors do not.

residual = observed - fitted. Plot the residuals against the fitted values, against each variable in the model, and against variables you left out.

The third instruction is the one people skip and the one that pays today. Session 2 found the funnel; this session finds a trend against a variable that is not in the model.

Lab 4.3, step 4

Household size and temperature travel together: that is confounding

Summer temperaturehot areas have larger households (5.19 people against 3.37 in mild areas)
↓

  • Correlation of household size with temperature: 0.38.
  • Comparing households by size alone compares, in part, hot areas with mild ones.
  • The one-variable slope credited people with litres that belong to the heat.
  • The move is predictable: 19.84 litres per degree × 0.619 degrees per additional person = 12.3.

A confounder is tied to both the variable of interest and the outcome. The arithmetic in the last bullet is the stretch of lab 4.3 and is exact for least squares. Temperature also pushes consumption directly, which the flow shows as one chain for simplicity; say so.

Which slope is right?

Both slopes are right, for different questions

The questionThe slopeWhy
How much more water for a family of 6 than of 4 in the coastal scheme?86 per person: about 172 litres a dayOne area, so temperature is held fixed

Jamila's scheme is in a mild coastal district, so her answer is 86 with its interval, 84 to 89. The 98 would have over-supplied the scheme by about 12 litres per person per day.

Lab 4.3, steps 3 and 5

The new residuals are flat; the list of confounders is never known to be complete

  • Trend of the new residuals in temperature: 0.0 litres per degree.
  • The funnel remains: hence the robust standard error.
  • A variable nobody measured may still be mixed in.

"Among households in areas of the same summer temperature, one more person goes with about 86 litres more a day (95% interval 84 to 89), and each degree with about 20 litres more. These are comparisons between different households, not the effect of adding a person to a home, and differences between areas that we did not measure may still be mixed into both numbers."

The card is the two-sentence interpretation the fifth course outcome asks for, from the solution run. The funnel in numbers: residual spread 40 litres for households of 1 or 2, 111 for 6 or more. In real data nobody hands you the generator. The second sentence is the limits statement. Only a designed comparison, where chance decides who gets what, removes confounders you did not think of; that is the subject of DS-302.

The five parts are the wording of the sixth course outcome: an estimate, an interval, a test, an effect size and stated limits. Days 1 to 4 each supplied one or two of them. This session assembles them on a question the room has not seen.

Before any computing

Turn the question into an estimand: one number in a named population

The manager saysThe estimand
"What share of repairs meet the 24-hour standard?"The share of this year's 12,000 repairs that took 24 hours or less
"Is one contractor better at it?"The difference between the contractors in that share
"Did the message reduce consumption?"The difference between message and control neighbourhoods in the mean change of litres a day per household
Write it before you open the data. With the unit you will resample and the comparisons you will make: that is the analysis plan of Day 3.

An estimand has a quantity, a population and, for a comparison, the two things compared. If the estimand cannot be written in one sentence, the question is not yet clear enough to analyse. The third row is one of the capstone questions.

Lab 4.4, step 3

One paragraph: estimate, interval, comparison, population, limits

"In a random sample of 400 of this year's 12,000 leak repairs, 94.5% were completed within the 24-hour standard (95% interval 92.0% to 96.5%). Contractor A met the standard more often than contractor B by 1.6 percentage points, a gap that could easily arise by chance (permutation p = 0.51; 95% interval -2.9 to +6.1 points). The population is the repairs logged this year at Zulal, including tickets closed late in the system. The sample cannot tell a late repair from a late entry, and it cannot show that the contractors are equal: a gap of a few points either way remains possible."

The solution's paragraph, lightly shortened for the screen. Point at each of the five parts. Two limits are stated: a data limit, late repair or late entry, from Day 1; and an inference limit, no difference shown is not equality, from Day 3.

The part most briefs leave out

Limits worth stating fall into four kinds

Population

Who the conclusion applies to: this year, these districts, surveyed households.

Sampling

How units were drawn, the unit resampled, what a sample this size cannot detect.

Measurement

What the recorded number is: a ticket closed late is not always a repair done late.

Comparison

Was assignment by lot or by rota, or are the groups different in other ways?

What goes wrong. A ritual sentence, "results should be interpreted with caution". A limit names what could be wrong and in which direction.

The responsible-use rule of the course belongs to the first card: every brief states the population its conclusions apply to, and results are given by district or contractor, never by household.

Checkpoint

Which draft is an inference brief?

A trial sent a reminder letter to 3,000 customers chosen by lot; 3,000 others got none. Which draft could a manager act on?

The letter worked: late payment differed significantly between the two groups of 3,000 (p = 0.004)
Late payment was 2.1 points lower among the customers who got the letter than among the others
We compared the groups with a permutation test and a bootstrap interval, both run with a fixed seed

Why. Estimate, interval, test and population are all there, to set against the cost of the letter.
