Session 1

The bootstrap idea

  • Explain why resampling the sample imitates sampling the population
  • Compute a percentile bootstrap interval for a median
  • Compare a bootstrap distribution with the real sampling distribution
01
The problem

A real analyst has one sample and no population to resample

  • Yesterday's sampling distributions came from drawing 1,000 samples from the population.
  • In real work you can afford one sample. The other 999 do not exist.
  • For a mean, a formula fills the gap: s / √n. For a median, a ratio or a slope there is often none.

Nizar's question

"What is the typical repair time, with an honest range around it?" You hold 150 tickets. Median 6.15 hours. How far could that be from the truth?

Sample and numbers from lab 2.1. Ask the room for ideas before the next slide. The idea needed is brazen: use the sample as a stand-in for the population.

The bootstrap idea

Treat the sample as the population and sample from it

1 · One sample150 repair times, the best picture of the population you have
→
2 · Resampledraw 150 from the 150, with replacement
→
3 · Recomputethe median of the resample
→
4 · Repeat2,000 times: an imitation of the sampling distribution
With replacement is the whole trick. Without it, every resample is the sample itself.

The name comes from "pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps": getting the uncertainty out of the sample that has it. The method needed cheap computing to become ordinary. Stress step 2: same size as the sample, with replacement.

Lab 2.1, step 2

One resample repeats some tickets and leaves others out

def resample(v, gen):
    return gen.choice(v, size=len(v),
                      replace=True)

one = resample(sample, gen)
len(np.unique(one))   # 77 distinct values
np.median(one)        # 6.40 hours
  • 150 values drawn, 77 distinct values. Many tickets appear twice or more; many do not appear.
  • Its median is 6.40 hours; the sample's was 6.15.
  • That difference imitates the difference between two real samples.

Solution run of lab 2.1. On average about 63% of the original values appear in a resample; with repeated values among the tickets the count of distinct values is lower. Step 2 of the lab is done by hand on purpose, before the vectorised version of step 3.

Lab 2.1, steps 3 and 4

The middle 95% of the resampled medians is the interval

2,000 bootstrap medians from one sample of 150 repairs.
  • Percentile interval: from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile, 5.00 to 7.50 hours.
  • The true median, 6.50 hours, is inside.
  • The histogram is lumpy: a median of 150 tickets can only take values in the sample, or halfway between two of them.

The sentence for Nizar: "The typical repair takes about 6.2 hours; the data are consistent with anything from 5.0 to 7.5." Do not say yet what 95% means: session 2 defines it and tests it.

Two numbers people confuse

More resamples smooth the picture; only more data narrow the interval

n, the sample sizeB, the number of resamples
What it is150 tickets you collected2,000 loops your laptop ran
What it costsField workSeconds
What more of it buysA narrower intervalA steadier estimate of the same interval
What goes wrong. "We ran a million resamples, so the result is very precise." The interval for 150 tickets is 2.5 hours wide at any B.

A practical rule for the labs: 1,000 to 2,000 resamples for an interval, 5,000 or more for a small p-value. The stretch of lab 2.1 counts the coverage of this interval method for the median: 958 of 1,000 fresh samples of 150, or 95.8%. Session 2 explains why that count is the real test of a method.

Checkpoint

Which procedure is a bootstrap resample?

You have 80 survey scores and want an interval for their median. Which step, repeated 2,000 times, gives the bootstrap distribution?

Shuffle the 80 scores and take their median
Draw 40 of the 80 scores at random and take their median
Draw 80 new scores from all customers and take their median
Draw 80 scores from the 80 with replacement and take their median
Why. Same size as the sample, from the sample, with replacement: that imitates drawing a new sample of 80 from the population.

Answer: D. Option A is the instructive one: ask what the 2,000 medians would look like. All equal. Shuffling without replacement returns on Day 3, where it is the right tool for a different job.

Session 2

Coverage is a testable claim

  • State what 95% coverage promises
  • Measure the coverage of two interval methods over simulated studies
  • Name two situations in which the bootstrap fails
02
What the number means

95% is a promise about the method, not about one interval

The promise

"Use this method on many studies and about 95 in 100 of the intervals it produces will contain the true value." That share is the coverage.

Not the promise

"There is a 95% chance that the truth is between 5.0 and 7.5." The truth is a fixed number. This one interval contains it or it does not.

At Zulal the promise can be checked. Draw 500 studies, build 500 intervals, count how many contain the truth.

The distinction sounds pedantic until the next slides, where the same method delivers 93%, 81% or 62% depending on the data. A promise about a method can be broken; this session counts when.

Lab 2.2, step 1

Two ways to an interval for a mean

The t interval

mean ± t × s / √n, with t about 2.02 for 40 tickets. Symmetric by construction.

One study of 40: 5.83 to 10.76 hours.

The bootstrap interval

Percentiles of 1,000 resampled means. Follows the skew of the sample.

The same study: 6.16 to 10.91 hours.

Mean of the 40 tickets: 8.29 hours. True mean of tickets closed on time: 8.86 hours. Both intervals contain it, in this study.

Solution run of lab 2.2. "In this study" is the phrase to lean on. One hit proves nothing about a method. The t interval is the classical one from any textbook; t rather than 2 allows for the fact that s is itself estimated.

Lab 2.2, step 2

Coverage is measured by a loop and a count

hits = 0
for _ in range(500):
    study = gen.choice(pool, size=40,
                       replace=False)
    low, high = t_interval(study)
    hits += low <= truth <= high
coverage = hits / 500
  • Each pass is one imaginary study, analysed the way you would analyse a real one.
  • The count has its own error: with 500 studies, a true 95% shows as 95% give or take 1.9 points.
  • The capstone asks for this loop on your own method.

The give or take is two standard errors of a share: the square root of 0.95 times 0.05 over 500, doubled. So 93.4% is not evidence of a broken method; 81% is. Make the room say which results on the next slide are outside the counting error.

Share of 500 studies whose 95% interval for the mean contained the true mean.

Rows 1 and 2: a point or two short, inside the counting error. Rows 3 and 4: one ticket in a thousand carries a tenth of all hours; most samples hold none, so most samples cannot know the true mean. More data make it worse, not better: the interval narrows around the wrong value until a late ticket finally appears. Neither method is at fault. The mean of this population is not learnable from small samples.

Know the limits

The bootstrap fails where the sample cannot stand in for the population

Rare extreme values

The sample usually misses them. Coverage for the mean fell to 62% (lab 2.2).

Statistics at the edge

A resample never exceeds the sample's maximum. Coverage for the maximum repair time: 1.3% (stretch).

Very small samples

Ten tickets hold too few distinct values to imitate a population. Intervals come out too short.

And one more, next session: resampling rows that were not sampled independently.

The honest summary of the method: it works for statistics from the middle of the data (means without wild tails, medians, differences, slopes) at moderate sample sizes, and you can check any particular use by simulation. Improved variants exist, for example BCa intervals; they are named here and not taught.

In a brief

When coverage fails, change the question or say so

  • Change the target: the median repair time, or the mean of tickets closed on time, with the late entries reported apart.
  • Say what the sample cannot know: "this sample contains no late-entry ticket; the mean over all tickets may be an hour higher."
  • Check your own method: a coverage count on data like yours, reported next to the interval.

The second bullet is a "limits" sentence of the kind the capstone brief requires. The population mean with late entries is 9.86 hours against 8.86 without: one hour. Day 1 already told them which summary answers "typical".

Sample of lab 2.3. This is how field surveys are usually done, to save travel. The district means run from 5.92 to 7.58: households of one district resemble each other. The file does not show how it was drawn; the analyst has to ask.

The hidden assumption

360 rows are not 360 independent pieces of information

  • Households of one district share the same pipes, crews and service office.
  • The 60th household of D05 tells you little that the first 59 did not.
  • Another survey would have visited six other districts. That is the largest source of variation, and the rows alone do not show it.

The estimate

6.60 from this sample.

Truth, all 9,000 responses: 6.67. The question is the width of the interval.

The technical name is clustering, and the households of one district are a cluster. The word is not needed; the question "what was drawn at random?" is.

Lab 2.3, steps 2 and 3

Same data, two resampling units, two different intervals

95% bootstrap intervals for mean satisfaction from the same 360 responses.
  • By household: resample the 360 rows. Width 0.38 points.
  • By district: resample the 6 districts, each with all its rows. Width 0.79 points.
  • Both contain the truth this time. Which width is honest?

The household bootstrap keeps the same six districts in every resample: it answers "what if we had phoned other households in these six districts?" The district bootstrap answers "what if we had run the survey again?", which is the question an interval is for.

The question to ask of every dataset

Design beats arithmetic. The same 360 calls spread over 12 districts of 30 give a narrower honest interval: 0.64 against 0.79 points (stretch).

In the capstone defence the assessor names a different resampling unit and the participant predicts what will happen. The prediction rule: a larger unit that groups similar rows widens the interval; if rows within the unit are no more alike than rows anywhere, nothing changes.

Lab 2.3, step 3

In code the change is one line: what you index

# by household: index the rows
idx = gen.integers(0, 360, size=(B, 360))
boot = scores[idx].mean(axis=1)
# by district: index the districts
dm = df.groupby("district")["satisfaction"].mean()
idx = gen.integers(0, 6, size=(B, 6))
boot = dm.to_numpy()[idx].mean(axis=1)

With 60 responses in every district, the mean of a resample of districts is the mean of their district means. With unequal counts, carry each drawn district's rows along.

The simplicity is the danger: both versions run, both print an interval, and nothing in the output says which is right. Only the knowledge of how the data were collected does.

Session 4

Intervals for differences and ratios

  • Compute a bootstrap interval for a difference between two groups
  • Compute a bootstrap interval for a ratio
  • Read an interval for a comparison against no difference
04
The question behind most questions

Managers rarely ask for a level; they ask for a gap

  • "Is contractor B slower than A, and by how much?"
  • A gap can be said two ways: a difference (hours) or a ratio (percent).
  • Either way it is a statistic, computed from two samples, with its own sampling distribution.
200 repairs eachmedian, hours
contractor A6.00
contractor B6.90
difference B - A0.90
ratio B / A1.15

Study of lab 2.4, tickets closed on time. Jobs go to the two contractors by rota at Zulal, so neither gets the harder jobs; that is what makes the comparison fair. In real data that has to be argued.

Lab 2.4, step 2

Resample each group on its own, then recompute the comparison

ia = gen.integers(0, len(a), size=(B, len(a)))
ib = gen.integers(0, len(b), size=(B, len(b)))
ma = np.median(a[ia], axis=1)
mb = np.median(b[ib], axis=1)

diff_boot  = mb - ma      # difference
ratio_boot = mb / ma      # ratio
  • The study fixed 200 repairs per contractor. Each resample keeps that.
  • One set of resamples serves both comparisons.
  • The interval is the percentile interval of the 2,000 differences, or ratios.

The recipe generalises to any comparison of two independent groups: resample each, recompute, repeat. The unit rule of session 3 still applies inside each group.

The honest report from this study of 400 tickets: "B looks slower, about 15%; the data do not rule out no difference, nor 44% slower." The truth is 24% slower. A study this size was simply not large enough to settle it. That sets up tomorrow: tests, and the power of a study.

Reading a comparison

An interval that includes "no gap" shows what is still possible, not that nothing is there

Wrong

"The interval includes 1, so the contractors are equally fast."

Right

"The study cannot tell B from A. It is consistent with B being 4% faster and with B being 44% slower."

And do not compare two separate intervals by eye. Whether the interval for A and the interval for B overlap is not a test. Build the interval for the difference itself.

Two separate 95% intervals can overlap although the interval for the difference excludes zero. The overlap rule of thumb is too conservative and is a common slip in dashboards with error bars.

Day 2 as one recipe

An interval for almost anything, in four questions

  1. What is the statistic? A median, a difference, a ratio: whatever answers the manager.
  2. What unit was drawn at random? Resample that unit, within each group.
  3. Where do the middle 95% of the resampled statistics lie? That is the interval.
  4. Does the method keep its promise on data like mine? Count the coverage.

Leave this slide up during the checkpoint if the room needs it. The fourth question is the one that separates this course from a recipe book.

Day 2 in three lines

What to keep

Resample the sample

With replacement, same size, many times. The middle 95% of the recomputed statistic is the interval.

Count the coverage

95% is a promise about the method. On a known population you can count whether it is kept: 93%, or 62%.

Resample the right unit

The unit that was drawn at random. Rows that share a district are not independent: 72% against 94%.
